Overview

Natural dyes produce the full spectrum of colors—from deep blues and greens to brilliant reds, yellows, and earth tones. Unlike industrial synthetic dyes, natural dyes are inherently slower to apply, require chemical baths (mordants), and demand skill to achieve consistent color. Yet they reward the dyer with subtle variations, unique character, and colors that improve with age and light exposure (rather than fading as badly as some synthetics).

The chemistry of natural dyeing involves understanding how mordants work (chemical bonding between fiber, dye molecule, and substrate), how pH shifts color, how vat dyes function without mordants, and how to layer dyes to create complex colors. A skilled natural dyer becomes part chemist, part farmer (growing or harvesting dye plants), and part artist.

This guide covers the foundational chemistry and comprehensive techniques for natural dyeing wool, linen, and other textiles.

Mordant Chemistry and Application

Mordants are chemical compounds that form a bond between the dye molecule and the fiber, fixing the color permanently.

How Mordants Work

A dye molecule by itself is often water-soluble but not fiber-binding. When applied to wet fiber, the dye molecules diffuse into the fiber but wash out easily. A mordant acts as a chemical "glue":

  1. Fiber bonding: The mordant binds strongly to the fiber's protein (in wool, silk) or cellulose (in linen, cotton, plant fiber) via chemical bonds.
  2. Dye bonding: The dye molecule bonds to the mordant through chemical attraction.
  3. Color shift: Different mordants shift the color of the same dye plant to different hues. For example, madder root produces red with alum, brown-orange with iron, and purple-black with tannin.

Primary Mordants

Alum (aluminum potassium sulfate, potash alum):

  • Effect: Brightens colors, increases wash and light fastness moderately.
  • Dose: 10–15% weight of fiber (e.g., 1.5 ounces alum per 1 pound of wool).
  • Process: Dissolve in hot water, add wet fiber, heat gently to 185°F (85°C) over 30 minutes, hold 1 hour, cool slowly overnight.
  • Best for: Yellow, red, orange dyes (madder, weld, cochineal, madder).
  • Toxicity: Low; alum is food-safe and used in food preservation.

Iron (ferrous sulfate):

  • Effect: Darkens colors dramatically, shifts reds toward brown and purple, improves wash fastness but can reduce light fastness if used excessively.
  • Dose: 0.1–0.5% weight of fiber (use with caution; even small amounts significantly alter color).
  • Process: Add dissolved iron salt to the dye vat in the final minutes of dyeing, or apply as an after-bath following a mordanted dye.
  • Best for: Darkening madder reds to burgundy or purple, deepening indigo blues to near-black, creating green tones (blue dye + yellow dye + iron).
  • Toxicity: Relatively safe in small amounts; excessive iron can cause adverse skin reactions.

Copper (copper sulfate):

  • Effect: Shifts colors toward green, yellow-green, and olive; moderate increase in fastness.
  • Dose: 0.1–0.2% weight of fiber.
  • Process: Add to the dye vat or apply as an after-bath.
  • Best for: Shifting yellow dyes to green-gold, olive tones with indigo or madder.
  • Toxicity: Copper is a heavy metal. Toxic in large amounts. Use sparingly and handle carefully.

Tannin (from oak galls, pomegranate rind, or sumac leaves):

  • Effect: Deepens colors, improves wash fastness significantly, shifts reds toward brown, creates dark tones with iron.
  • Dose: 2–10% weight of fiber depending on source (oak galls are strong; sumac is weaker).
  • Process: Apply as a separate mordant bath before or after the dye, or add to the dye vat directly.
  • Best for: Setting madder, deepening all warm colors, creating brown-black tones when combined with iron.
  • Toxicity: Safe; tannins are found in tea and wine.

Mordant Application Sequence

Alum pre-mordant (most common):

  1. Clean the fiber or yarn (see Spinning guide, Fiber Preparation).
  2. Dissolve alum in hot water and add the damp fiber.
  3. Slowly heat to 185°F and hold for 1 hour.
  4. Remove from heat and cool slowly (this allows the fiber to absorb the mordant evenly).
  5. Once cool, use the mordanted fiber in the dye vat immediately or store until ready.

After-mordant (iron darkening):

  1. Dye the fiber with the dye vat (see Dye Vat Preparation, below).
  2. Remove the dyed fiber and cool slightly.
  3. Add iron solution to a separate bath and immerse the warm dyed fiber for 5–15 minutes, stirring gently.
  4. The color shifts and darkens within seconds.
  5. Cool and rinse.

Tannin mordant:

  1. Apply tannin bath before or after dyeing depending on desired effect.
  2. As a pre-mordant: heat fiber gently in a tannin bath for 30 minutes, cool, then dye.
  3. As an after-bath: dye first, then immerse in a hot tannin bath and observe color deepening.

Some mordants (especially copper and iron) are toxic. Wear gloves, don't inhale fumes, and store solutions carefully labeled. Never use mordant dye vessels for food preparation.

Vat Dyes and the Indigo Vat Process

Vat dyes (of which indigo is the most famous) require no mordant. Instead, they exist in an insoluble form until chemically reduced to a soluble form, during which they bond directly to fiber.

Indigo Chemistry

Indigo (indigofera tinctoria plant, or synthetic indigo) is insoluble in water—a fine blue powder. To use it as a dye, it must be "reduced" to a soluble form using an alkaline reducing agent.

Classic reduction methods:

  1. Thiourea dioxide (TRO) vat:

    • Modern, effective, relatively safe.
    • Indigo + thiourea dioxide + sodium hydroxide (lye) + water.
    • Proportions: 1 ounce indigo, 1/2 ounce thiourea dioxide, 1 ounce sodium hydroxide per gallon of water.

  2. Fermentation vat (traditional):

    • Indigo + bran or other carbohydrate + alkaline substance (wood ash lye, ammonia from aged urine).
    • Fermentation produces organic reducing agents.
    • Slower (takes days to establish) but effective once established.
    • Produces a yellowish froth on the surface ("flower") that indicates a healthy vat.

  3. Sodium hydrosulfite vat:

    • Indigo + sodium hydrosulfite + sodium hydroxide.
    • Very effective but sodium hydrosulfite smells bad (like rotten eggs).

Indigo Vat Preparation (Thiourea Dioxide Method)

  1. Prepare the vat:

    • Fill a glass, ceramic, or stainless steel pot with 1 gallon of warm water (120–140°F).
    • Dissolve indigo powder in a separate small pot of hot water, stirring thoroughly (some indigo will remain suspended; this is normal).
    • Pour the indigo slurry into the main pot.

  2. Add reducing agent and alkali:

    • Dissolve thiourea dioxide in a small amount of hot water.
    • Slowly add sodium hydroxide (lye) solution to the main pot while stirring gently. The vat will turn from blue to yellow-green; this indicates successful reduction.
    • Add the thiourea dioxide solution.
    • Stir well but gently (excessive agitation re-oxidizes the indigo).

  3. Rest the vat:

    • Let the vat sit undisturbed for 15–30 minutes until the indigo settles to the bottom and a clear golden liquid appears on top.

Dyeing with the Indigo Vat

  1. Prepare fiber:

    • Pre-wet the fiber or yarn in warm water (no mordant needed).

  2. Enter the vat:

    • Lower the wet fiber gently into the warm vat, sinking it below the surface.
    • Gently turn the fiber over every few minutes to ensure even color. Avoid unnecessary agitation.
    • Dye time: 10–30 minutes depending on desired shade (longer = darker blue).

  3. Remove from vat:

    • Lift the fiber gently and allow excess vat to drip back.
    • The fiber will be yellowish-green at first.

  4. Oxidation:

    • Hang the fiber in air for 10–15 minutes. The yellow-green gradually shifts to deep blue as the indigo oxidizes and bonds permanently to the fiber.

  5. Rinse and repeat:

    • Once fully blue, rinse in cool water.
    • For deeper shades, repeat the process (dye, oxidize) multiple times.
    • Each dip adds another layer of indigo.

A well-maintained indigo vat can be used repeatedly for months or years. Store it covered and undisturbed. Before each use, stir gently to re-dissolve the indigo, add a small amount of reducing agent to restore the vat's reducing power, and rest for 15 minutes.

Dye Plant Sources and Color Yields

Over 20 plant species provide useful dyes. Availability depends on region and season.

Yellow Dyes

Weld (Reseda luteola) — Bright golden yellow

  • Plant part: Whole plant (stems, leaves, flowers) at flowering time.
  • Yield: High; produces strong, bright yellow.
  • Fastness: Excellent light and wash fastness.
  • Note: One of the most reliable and prized yellow dyes. A historical favorite in Europe.

Coreopsis (Coreopsis tinctoria) — Golden yellow

  • Plant part: Dried flowers.
  • Yield: Moderate.
  • Fastness: Good.
  • Note: Easy to grow; many ornamental varieties contain dye.

Goldenrod (Solidago spp.) — Bright yellow to mustard

  • Plant part: Flowering tops.
  • Yield: Moderate to high; abundant wild plant.
  • Fastness: Good.
  • Note: Often underestimated; produces reliable yellows.

Cutch (Acacia catechu) — Deep yellow to brown-yellow

  • Plant part: Heartwood extract.
  • Yield: High concentration.
  • Fastness: Excellent.
  • Note: Import; used as a tannin source as well.

Red and Pink Dyes

Madder Root (Rubia tinctorum) — Red, orange, brown, purple depending on mordant

  • Plant part: Root (3+ years old; older roots are more concentrated).
  • Yield: Moderate; requires careful extraction.
  • Fastness: Excellent light and wash fastness.
  • Note: The classic red dye. Production is labor-intensive but the color is worth it. Reds appear with alum mordant; orange-red with tannin; purple-red with iron.

Cochineal (Dactylopius coccus — insect dye) — Bright crimson, scarlet, pink

  • Plant part: Dried insect bodies.
  • Yield: Very high; small amount produces strong color.
  • Fastness: Excellent.
  • Note: Pre-Columbian dye; imported from Central America. Expensive but powerful.

Safflower (Carthamus tinctorius) — Pink to red

  • Plant part: Flower petals.
  • Yield: Moderate; requires multiple extraction steps.
  • Fastness: Good on wool, poor on cotton.
  • Note: Complex chemistry; produces pink (acid-extracted) or red (alkaline-extracted) depending on pH.

Lichen (Rocella tinctoria, Ochrolechia, Pertusaria) — Purple, violet

  • Plant part: Whole lichen.
  • Yield: Moderate; requires aging (months) with ammonia or stale urine.
  • Fastness: Good.
  • Note: Historical Tyrian purple. Slow process but produces distinctive purple-violet.

Blue Dyes

Indigo (Indigofera tinctoria) — Deep blue

  • Plant part: Leaves (processed to extract indigo pigment).
  • Yield: Moderate; requires fermentation extraction.
  • Fastness: Excellent.
  • Note: See Vat Dyes section. No mordant needed.

Woad (Isatis tinctoria) — Blue (weaker than indigo)

  • Plant part: Leaves, especially first-year plants.
  • Yield: Moderate; lower yield of indigo than Indigofera.
  • Fastness: Good.
  • Note: European historical dye; can be grown in cool climates. Often blended with indigo to reduce cost.

Green Dyes

Greens are traditionally created by overdyeing blue with yellow rather than using a single dye source.

Japanese Indigo (Polygonum tinctorum) — Blue, greens with overdye

  • Faster-growing relative of indigo.
  • Plant part: Leaves.
  • Yield: Similar to woad.
  • Note: Annual plant; easier to grow than Indigofera in cool regions.

Brown and Earth Tones

Walnut Hulls (Juglans regia) — Dark brown to chocolate

  • Plant part: Green outer hull (before nut matures).
  • Yield: Very high; abundant in autumn.
  • Fastness: Excellent.
  • Note: One of the easiest and most productive dyes. Can stain hands black; use gloves.

Oak Galls (Quercus spp.) — Brown, gray, olive

  • Plant part: Galls (abnormal growths on leaves caused by insects).
  • Yield: High; concentrated tannin source.
  • Fastness: Excellent.
  • Note: Primarily a mordant (tannin), but can produce brown tones independently. Also deepens all colors.

Pomegranate Rind (Punica granatum) — Deep brown to black with iron

  • Plant part: Dried rind.
  • Yield: Moderate.
  • Fastness: Good to excellent with tannin.
  • Note: Primarily a tannin; shifts colors toward brown-black.

Resist Techniques and Tie-Dye Patterns

Resist techniques prevent dye from reaching certain areas of the fiber, creating patterns.

Tie-Resist (Tie-Dye)

  1. Tie the fiber with cotton thread at regular intervals:

    • Wrap thread tightly around the yarn or cloth at points 1–3 inches apart.
    • Wrap multiple times at each point to fully compress the fiber and prevent dye penetration.
    • Each wrap creates a circular resist area.

  2. Dye as normal, then rinse and hang to dry.

  3. Untie to reveal white (undyed) circles on the colored background.

Variations:

  • Stripes: Tie along the length of the yarn at regular intervals perpendicular to the yarn length.
  • Dots: Tie single knots along the yarn.
  • Bands: Tie the entire yarn at one point.

Wax-Resist (Batik)

  1. Apply melted beeswax or paraffin to cloth using a brush, stamp, or dipping.
  2. Dye the cloth. The wax resists dye penetration.
  3. Remove the wax by melting (over low heat, with a cloth underneath to absorb melted wax) or by washing in hot water and detergent.

Advantages:

  • Fine detail possible.
  • Multiple colors possible by applying wax, dyeing, removing wax, and repeating with different colors.

Disadvantages:

  • Labor-intensive.
  • Wax disposal is necessary (don't pour down drains).

Starch-Resist

  1. Apply starch paste (made from flour, potato starch, or rice bran) to cloth using a brush or block.
  2. Allow to dry.
  3. Dye the cloth. Starch resists dye.
  4. Rinse thoroughly to remove starch.

Advantages:

  • Eco-friendly; starch biodegrades.
  • Quick to apply with stamps or blocks.

Disadvantages:

  • Less precise than wax; edges are softer.

Clamp-Resist

  1. Fold or bunch the cloth and hold in place with clamps, clothespins, or by tying.
  2. Dye the exposed areas.
  3. Unclamp to reveal the resist pattern.

Patterns:

  • Concentric circles: Bunch the center and clamp vertically.
  • Stripes: Fold the cloth in accordion folds and clamp along the fold.
  • Geometric shapes: Fold into triangles or squares and clamp.

Overdyeing and Color Layering

Overdyeing is the process of applying successive dye baths to create complex, blended colors.

Overdyeing for Color Depth

  1. First dye: Dye fiber with a primary dye (e.g., yellow weld).
  2. Second dye: Dye the same fiber with an overdye bath (e.g., indigo vat).
  3. Result: The two colors visually blend. Yellow + blue = green (depth depends on concentration of each dye).

Overdyeing for Variation

Historic dyers used overdyeing to achieve subtle hue variations without using multiple dye sources:

  1. Base dye: Dye fiber with madder to produce red.
  2. Indigo overdye (light): A brief indigo dip shifts red to red-purple.
  3. Iron shift: A light iron after-bath darkens the red without fully shifting it to brown.

Result: A complex red-purple tone impossible with a single dye.

Overdye Calculations

When overdyeing, the resulting color depends on both dye concentrations:

  • Light yellow + light blue = green
  • Light yellow + dark blue = dark green
  • Dark yellow + light blue = olive
  • Dark red + light blue = red-purple
  • Dark red + dark indigo = deep burgundy

Test overdyes on sample yarns before committing to the full batch.

Lightfastness Testing and Assessment

Lightfastness measures how well a dyed fabric resists fading when exposed to sunlight.

AATCC Lightfastness Scale

The American Association of Textile Chemists and Colorists defines a standard 5-point scale:

Scale Rating Persistence
5 Excellent No visible change after extended sun exposure
4 Good Minimal change; still acceptable for most uses
3 Fair Noticeable but gradual fading; acceptable for indoor use
2 Poor Fading visible within weeks
1 Very Poor Severe fading within days

DIY Lightfastness Testing

  1. Create test samples: Dye multiple small swatches of the same fiber with the same dye.
  2. Expose one sample: Pin or tape the swatch to a window with good sunlight exposure.
  3. Control sample: Keep an identical swatch in a dark drawer (this remains as the baseline, undyed reference).
  4. Assess at intervals: Compare the exposed swatch to the control at 1 week, 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 8 weeks, and 12 weeks.
  5. Rate the change: Estimate the percentage of color change and assign a AATCC scale rating.

Interpretation:

  • Scale 4–5 dyes: Excellent for all uses; fade is imperceptible.
  • Scale 3 dyes: Good for indoor use (curtains, upholstery) but not ideal for outdoor textiles or items exposed to strong direct sunlight.
  • Scale 1–2 dyes: Decorative use only; expect significant fading.

Factors Affecting Lightfastness

Factor Effect
Dye type Indigo and madder are highly light-fast; cochineal and safflower less so
Mordant Alum + tannin improves fastness over alum alone
pH during dye Acidic conditions (lower pH) generally improve light fastness
Fiber type Wool retains dyed color better than cotton; protein fibers absorb mordant better
Light type UV-rich sunlight fades faster; northern light exposure less damaging than southern

Color Stability and Fastness Ratings

This table rates each dye source on three fastness properties, allowing you to select dyes appropriate for their intended use (clothing vs. decorative items) and choose mordant/fiber combinations that maximize durability.

Fastness Scales (1–5)

Wash Fastness: How well the color resists detergent, water, and abrasion during laundry.

  • 1: Severe fading with one wash
  • 2: Significant fading after 5 washes
  • 3: Noticeable fading after 20 washes; acceptable for decorative items
  • 4: Slight fading after 50 washes; good for everyday clothing
  • 5: No visible fading even after many washes; excellent for durable clothing

Light Fastness: How well color resists UV exposure and sunlight.

  • 1: Severe fading within days of sun exposure
  • 2: Noticeable fading within weeks
  • 3: Gradual fading over months; acceptable for indoor textiles
  • 4: Minimal fading over 1+ years of normal exposure
  • 5: Essentially no fading under normal use; excellent for outdoor or long-term display

Rub Fastness: How well color resists surface abrasion and crocking (color transfer to hands or other items).

  • 1: Severe crocking immediately; color rubs off easily
  • 2: Significant color transfer with rubbing; avoid touching other fabrics
  • 3: Moderate crocking; acceptable for garments that don't receive heavy wear
  • 4: Minimal crocking; safe for most applications
  • 5: No crocking even with vigorous rubbing; excellent for all uses

Mordant Combinations for Maximum Fastness

Different mordant combinations significantly improve overall durability. Use these recipes for items requiring exceptional longevity (clothing, blankets, important textiles).

For red dyes (madder, cochineal):

  1. Pre-mordant: Alum (10–15% weight of fiber) at 185°F for 1 hour
  2. Dye: In madder vat at 160–180°F for 1 hour
  3. After-mordant: Tannin (2–4% weight of fiber) at 160°F for 30 minutes, then iron (0.1% weight of fiber) for 2–5 minutes

Result: Wash fastness 5, Light fastness 5, Rub fastness 4–5. Cost: moderate (requires multiple dye baths).

For yellow dyes (weld, goldenrod):

  1. Pre-mordant: Alum + cream of tartar (1:1 ratio, total 12% fiber weight)
  2. Dye: In weld or goldenrod vat at 160°F for 1 hour
  3. After-mordant: Tannin (2% fiber weight) at 160°F for 30 minutes

Result: Wash fastness 4–5, Light fastness 4, Rub fastness 4. Cost: low (single post-mordant).

For blue and green dyes (indigo, woad):

  1. Dye: Vat dye (indigo/woad) without mordant; multiple dips for darker shade
  2. After-mordant: Iron (0.1% fiber weight) for darkening/shifting to green tones

Result: Wash fastness 5, Light fastness 5, Rub fastness 5. Cost: very low (no pre-mordant; iron optional).

For brown and earth tones (walnut, oak galls):

  1. Pre-mordant: Tannin (2–4% fiber weight) at 160°F for 30 minutes
  2. Dye: In walnut or oak gall vat at 160–180°F for 45–60 minutes
  3. After-mordant: Iron (0.1–0.2% fiber weight) for 2–5 minutes (darkens to nearly black)

Result: Wash fastness 5, Light fastness 3–4, Rub fastness 4. Cost: very low (tannins inexpensive; often abundant wild material).

Testing methods for fastness improvement:

To verify that your mordant combination improved fastness:

  1. Wash test: Dye multiple samples with and without the after-mordant. Wash identically 10 times. Compare color depth and fading.
  2. Light test: Expose samples to direct sunlight (or bright window) for 4 weeks. Compare fading rate.
  3. Rub test: Dampen a white cloth and rub vigorously on the dyed sample. Measure color transfer by comparing the cloth to a control.

Keep detailed notes: date, dye source, fiber type, mordant sequence, and fastness ratings. Over time, you'll develop reliable recipes for specific purposes.

Natural dyeing is both an art and a science. The rewards—unique colors, sustainable practice, and the satisfaction of creating color from plants—justify the time investment. A dyer who understands chemistry, plants, and fiber can produce textiles of lasting beauty.
